Fiona sent over Gresham House’s third-quarter numbers with a note: “Revenue up 22%, asset value up again. Storage is fine, then?” [[1]](https://www.investegate.co.uk/announcement/rns/gresham-house-energy-storage-fund--grid/q3-2026-quarterly-nav-announcement/9878255)

The batteries are doing their job. The valuation bridge tells a rather different story.

The fund knocked 3.08p per share off its valuation because revenue forecasts had fallen, largely on lower near-term gas prices. Another 3.39p disappeared when it raised its discount rate by a quarter of a percentage point to reflect higher gilt yields [[1]](https://www.investegate.co.uk/announcement/rns/gresham-house-energy-storage-fund--grid/q3-2026-quarterly-nav-announcement/9878255).

In one quarter, the bond market did more damage to the valuation of Britain’s largest battery fund than the power market.

True, though the scale is worth examining. A quarter-point increase in the discount rate cost roughly 2.5% of net asset value. That is the sort of sensitivity you would expect from a long-dated bond. Much of a merchant battery’s value depends on cash flows a decade or more away, where even a small change in the discount rate has a substantial effect.

The Scottish deals announced this week are interesting in that light. The National Wealth Fund is committing up to £87m of equity alongside Matrix at Eccles and Kilmarnock, two projects totalling 1 GW [[2]](https://www.solarpowerportal.co.uk/battery-storage/national-wealth-fund-backs-nationally-significant-1gw-2gwh-of-scottish-bess). Macquarie is investing alongside Revera at Windyhill and Hunterston [[3]](https://revera.energy/news/revera-and-macquarie-capital-form-strategic-jv). Three of the four projects already have bank debt in place and are under construction [[3]](https://revera.energy/news/revera-and-macquarie-capital-form-strategic-jv) [[4]](https://www.energyglobal.com/energy-storage/24092026/revera-energy-achieves-financial-close-and-issues-ntp-on-hunterston-bess/) [[5]](https://www.ess-news.com/2026/11/20/uk-state-investor-buys-into-2-gwh-of-scottish-battery-storage/).

That matters. Equity coming in at this stage faces fewer development risks and can reasonably accept a lower return. Scotland needs the capacity, too, as more wind generation adds to an already difficult balancing problem.

Fiona’s next question would probably be: “And what happens to developers without a state-backed investor on the cap table?”

Put a few megawatts of containerised compute near a wind node where turbines are regularly curtailed, with six to eight hours of battery storage alongside it. Secure a compute customer before committing the capital. The servers can then begin earning as soon as they are commissioned, with electricity procured close to where surplus generation is available. The hardware needs to pay for itself within a GPU generation rather than over the battery’s fifteen-year life.

A smaller proportion of the investment’s value depends on revenue in year twelve, making it less exposed to a quarter-point move in discount rates.

“You’ve swapped interest-rate risk for technology-refresh risk.”

To an extent. GPUs become obsolete quickly, and compute prices can fall sharply. The difference is that hardware can be replaced and commercial terms reset every few years. That gives an operator opportunities to respond to changing economics. A battery valued against a fifteen-year merchant revenue forecast offers much less room to adjust.

It also changes what the investment committee has to underwrite. A compute-and-storage project needs a credible customer contract, realistic hardware depreciation and a plan for each refresh cycle.
