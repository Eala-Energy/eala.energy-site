Ewan forwarded the Gresham House announcement last week with one line: “Britain’s largest listed battery fund puts itself up for sale. Bad sign for storage?” [[1]](https://quoteddata.com/2026/11/gresham-house-energy-storage-puts-itself-up-for-sale/)

“I’d say it’s a bad sign for the way the fund owns storage.”

“It has over a gigawatt across some thirty sites. That’s as diversified as UK storage gets.” [[2]](https://greshamhouse.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Gresham-House-Energy-Storage-Fund-plc-Factsheet-31-March-2026.pdf)[[3]](https://greshamhouse.com/row/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRID-Annual-Review_25_20.04-FINAL.pdf)

Geographically, perhaps. The trouble is that nearly every battery depends on the same few revenue streams: wholesale spreads, the Balancing Mechanism and a shrinking pool of ancillary service contracts. When ancillary prices collapsed in 2023, they fell everywhere at once [[4]](https://modoenergy.com/research/en/dynamic-containment-low-prices-post-saturation).

Over the three years to March 2026, the shares lost about half their value, including dividends. Net asset value fell by less than a quarter [[2]](https://greshamhouse.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Gresham-House-Energy-Storage-Fund-plc-Factsheet-31-March-2026.pdf)[[5]](https://greshamhouse.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Gresham-House-Energy-Storage-Fund-plc-Factsheet-31-March-2023-vF-2.pdf).

“Hence the activist, and the sale.” [[6]](https://quoteddata.com/2026/07/activist-primestone-requests-outright-sale-of-gresham-house-energy-storage-to-unlock-140p-per-share-of-value/)

A private buyer may well pay something closer to NAV. I can see the attraction. But whoever buys the portfolio inherits those same revenue exposures. Thirty batteries trading in one market can behave remarkably like one large position when conditions turn against them.

“So what would you own instead?”

I’d look at sites that can earn money in more than one way.

Take a wind node in northern Scotland, where turbines are regularly curtailed because the transmission lines south are full. Put a few megawatts of containerised compute there, with six to eight hours of battery storage alongside it. Add software that forecasts wind output and decides where each megawatt-hour is worth most: running servers, charging the battery or selling a grid service.

The compute has a customer and a revenue model that don’t depend directly on GB wholesale spreads. Curtailment also varies from site to site, depending on local weather and network constraints. That gives you rather more diversification than simply adding another battery to the same trading portfolio.

“Compute has its own cycle.”

Certainly. Demand can fall, equipment becomes obsolete and customers have their own credit risks. I’m not suggesting those risks disappear. But they are different risks, and a portfolio of ten sites with several sources of income could have a rather different correlation profile from ten conventional grid batteries.

“And the grid batteries?”

We need more of them, and we need them sooner. As wind capacity grows, the system needs more flexibility, particularly where generation is concentrated behind constrained transmission lines. Batteries have a useful role in providing it.

I’d still invest in standalone BESS. I would simply be more careful about treating geographical diversification as revenue diversification.

The question I’d put to a portfolio manager is how often one of their sites has a bad month while the others are doing well. If the answer is hardly ever, I’d want to understand just how diversified that portfolio really is.
